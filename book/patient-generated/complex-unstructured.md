The patient-generated streams covered so far—glucose, heart rate, steps, weight—share a comforting property: they are numbers, arriving on a clock, with a unit. This final category of patient-generated data has none of that tidiness. It is complex, unstructured, often unvalidated, and ethically fraught, and it is where the frontier of patient data is moving fastest. Two forms are emerging as genuinely consequential.

## Image & Voice Data

Patients now carry, in their pockets, a high-resolution camera and a sensitive microphone—and both are turning into clinical instruments.

Image data generated by patients is already in routine use at the edges of care. Teledermatology runs on patient-submitted photos of skin lesions and rashes; wound-care and post-surgical programs ask patients to photograph healing incisions; and smartphone attachments and apps capture retinal images, otoscope views, and more. The appeal is obvious—a photo can substitute for a visit—but so are the pitfalls: lighting, focus, and framing vary wildly, color is uncalibrated, and models trained on clinician-captured images degrade on patient-captured ones. And the same demographic bias that haunts pulse oximetry haunts image models: dermatology algorithms trained predominantly on lighter skin tones perform worse on darker ones.[^1]

Voice data is the more speculative and more intriguing case. The idea of the vocal biomarker is that *how* a person speaks—prosody, pauses, jitter, articulation—carries signal about their health independent of what they say. Research has explored voice features as markers for depression, Parkinson's disease, cognitive decline, and respiratory conditions[^2], and the ambient scribes already woven through this book's clinical vignettes mean microphones are increasingly present in the exam room anyway. The promise is passive, zero-effort screening; the reality as of 2026 is mostly research-grade, with few validated, deployed clinical products—and voice is about as identifying and sensitive as data gets, which brings us to the last category.

## Social & Digital Interaction

The broadest and most ethically charged frontier is the digital exhaust of ordinary life. Under the banner of digital phenotyping, researchers have studied whether patterns in digital communication, smartphone usage, typing dynamics, GPS mobility, and app engagement correlate with mental-health states and disease trajectories.[^3] Closer to the clinic, patient-portal messages, appointment and refill behavior, and engagement with health apps are increasingly mined as signals of adherence, deterioration, and disengagement. And further out, consumer data that was never meant to be health data at all—search history, purchases, wearables' location traces—can be startlingly predictive of health events.

Two things distinguish this category from everything else in the book. First, almost none of it is validated or standardized; it is signal-rich and interpretation-poor, and the risk of spurious correlation is enormous. Second, and more importantly, it sits mostly *outside* the protections that govern clinical data. Data brokered from apps and consumer platforms is generally not covered by HIPAA, so the privacy regime that this book's de-identification chapter describes may not apply at all. The technology to infer health from digital behavior is running well ahead of the ethical and regulatory frameworks to govern it, and any practitioner working with this data should treat the ethical questions as primary, not as a compliance afterthought.
